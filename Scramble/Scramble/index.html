How it Works?

→
output Ciphertext Salt + Nonce + Data, Base64

01 · Your password becomes a key

AES needs a proper binary key, not a word you can remember. PBKDF2 stretches your password through 200,000 rounds of hashing to produce one and makes guessing it far slower for an attacker.

02 · You pick how strong the key Size

128, 192, or 256-bit keys mean 10, 12, or 14 rounds of scrambling respectively. All three are secure today. A longer key just adds extra margin against future attacks.

03 · GCM adds a tamper seal

A random nonce plus an authentication tag are attached to every message. If even one bit of the ciphertext changes, decryption fails loudly instead of silently returning garbage.

04 · Decryption reverses and verifies

The same password re-derives the same key, and the key size is read automatically from the ciphertext. Wrong password or altered data? AES-GCM refuses to produce output so it never guesses.

Why AES changes the game?

The Caesar cipher had 25 possible keys. AES-GCM has more combinations than there are atoms in the observable universe.
Here's what that actually means.

Caesar Cipher 25 possible keys
cracked in < under seconds, by hand if needed
AES-128-GCM 2¹²⁸ possible keys
infeasible to brute-force
AES-256-GCM 2²⁵⁶ possible keys
The largest practical safety margin available nowadays
strength

Enormous key Space

Even a supercomputer trying trillions of keys per second would need far longer to exhaust the possibilities at any of the three sizes.

strength

Authenticated, not just hidden

GCM mode detects tampering. A Caesar cipher can't tell if someone altered the ciphertext. AES-GCM refuses to decrypt it at all.

Real weak point

Your password

AES itself isn't the weak link anymore. A short, guessable password is. "password123" under AES-256 is still a bad password. Choose something long and unique.
